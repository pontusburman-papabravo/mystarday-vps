Feeling cards for children — free

Young children feel big emotions but have a small feeling vocabulary. Feeling cards give you shared language: your child can point to “tired” instead of only crying, and you can respond without guessing wrong. Download our free cards below.

How to use feeling cards at home

Start with two or three cards — happy, sad, angry — and add more when your child recognises them. Keep the cards where they are easy to reach: on the fridge, in a box by the sofa, or in your child’s room. They should be used in the moment, not filed away in a binder.

When your child points to a card, reflect the feeling: “You’re tired, not angry. That’s okay.” Validation before fixing builds safety. Over time, many children learn to pick the right card before frustration boils over.

Feeling cards in nursery and school

Educators use feeling cards at circle time, after conflicts, or when a child needs a break. A visual feelings wheel on the wall reminds everyone that all feelings are allowed — including the hard ones. Children with autism or ADHD often need that visual step between a body reaction and words.

Laminate the cards if they are handled often. Or print fresh copies when they wear out — our templates are free and always available.

  • Reflect the feeling before suggesting a fix
  • Combine with a calm corner or break space when needed

From cards to everyday routines

Feeling cards do not solve every situation — they open the conversation. Pair them with clear routines so your child knows what comes after a difficult moment. An evening schedule can help them recover after an emotional afternoon.

When feeling cards are not enough

Feeling cards help your child name the emotion — not always regulate it. If they point to “angry” but keep throwing things, they often need a break, physical activity, or a predictable routine afterwards.

Combine cards with calm zones and clear boundaries. The cards start the talk; adults still set the limits on what is okay to do with those feelings.
