What is a visual schedule?

A visual schedule shows activities as pictures, symbols, or photos instead of a paragraph of instructions. The child sees what happens now and next, and can tick each step. For many children that is the difference between waiting to be told and knowing what comes.

Schedules are used at home, in preschool, and at school. They can be five steps — toilet, wash hands, breakfast — or a bigger step split into smaller ones. The point is the same: make an ordinary sequence concrete.

A daily schedule covers one day. A weekly visual schedule shows how the days differ, such as sport on Tuesday or a different house on Wednesday. Both can live in the same app. The child’s view should stay visual. Words can sit beside the pictures for adults.

Why picture support works

Young children, and children who need more structure, often take in a sequence faster as pictures than as speech. “Get ready” is vague. A picture for each step is not.

A visible list also lightens working memory. Instead of remembering ten morning jobs, the child looks at the next picture. Transitions get clearer because the next activity is already on the screen. The same pictures in the same order, morning after morning, are what turn a chart into a habit.

Ticking a step is a small, concrete “I did that”. It is not a treatment, and it will not suit every child the same way. It is a tool, like a list you would write for yourself.

Who it is for

  • Young children who are not reading fluently and still want to do it themselves.
  • Chaotic mornings where everyone forgets the next step.
  • Children who lose the thread and need a clear “this one now”.
  • Siblings who need different schedules in the same house.
  • New routines when school, a club, or a house change lands.

A visual schedule is not only for children with a diagnosis. Families supporting ADHD or autism often use one because predictability matters. My Starday does not treat either, and it does not promise a clinical result.

Example: a morning

Five to seven steps

  1. Wake up and get out of bed
  2. Toilet and wash hands
  3. Get dressed
  4. Breakfast
  5. Brush teeth
  6. Bag, coat, shoes
  7. Ready to leave

Start shorter if the morning feels too big. Three steps that happen beat ten that do not. More detail is in the morning routine guide. For bedtime, print the evening schedule PDF or build the same block in the app.

Paper or app

A visual schedule board on the wall is excellent when the plan stays in one room. Printables in the resource library cover mornings, weeks, reward charts, and first-then cards. The app is for families who do not want to reprint when swimming moves from Tuesday to Wednesday, and who want stars tied to the steps. Stars are earned inside the routine. They are not something you buy.
